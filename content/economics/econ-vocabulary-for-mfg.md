## Article

The aim is to talk with economists who might use mean field games, not to learn all of economics. Most of the mathematics is familiar. The framing and the vocabulary are what differ.

### Equilibrium means fixed point

Economists usually start from an **equilibrium concept**, not from a PDE. Agents optimize while taking something as given (prices, the distribution of other agents, others' strategies), and that something is itself the outcome of everyone's optimization. So "equilibrium" means a **fixed point of a best-response map**. It does not mean "steady state" in the ODE sense: a dynamic equilibrium can be a whole time path. The two get identified only after a model is written recursively and stationarity is imposed (see [Infinite horizon is not stationary](#/note/infinite-horizon-vs-steady-state)). Learning to see a fixed point as an MFG consistency condition is most of the translation work.

### General equilibrium and the welfare theorems

An **Arrow–Debreu (competitive) equilibrium** is a price vector at which all markets clear simultaneously, given that every consumer and firm optimizes at those prices. This fixed point in prices is the finite-agent ancestor of the MFG loop: optimal control given the field, then the field regenerated by aggregating the controls.

The two **welfare theorems** are the results economists cite most often. They have different hypotheses:

- **First:** if preferences are locally nonsatiated (and markets are complete, with no externalities), every competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient. No convexity is needed.
- **Second:** with convex preferences and technologies (plus continuity), every Pareto efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium after lump-sum transfers. Convexity is essential here, since the proof uses a separating hyperplane.

### Nonatomic games: the economists' genealogy of MFG

Economists already have a static theory of games with a continuum of players. Aumann (1964) studied markets with a continuum of traders. Schmeidler (1973) proved existence of equilibrium in nonatomic games, and Mas-Colell (1984, "On a theorem of Schmeidler") restated it for anonymous games, where a player's payoff depends on their own action and the *distribution* of others' actions. Each player is negligible and optimizes against that distribution. An economist will place MFG as **the dynamic, stochastic-control version of nonatomic game theory**, and that framing is the right one to use.

### Dynamic programming in the economist's dialect

The HJB equation appears as
$$
\rho V(a) = \max_c \{\, u(c) + V'(a)\,(y + ra - c) \,\}.
$$
Here $\rho$ is the discount rate, the state $a$ is wealth (assets), the control $c$ is consumption, $y$ is income and $r$ the interest rate. Economists impose a **transversality condition** where an analyst would impose a growth or boundary condition at infinity.

The "hello world" of macro is the **Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans** growth model, a deterministic control problem: choose consumption to maximize $\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t}u(c_t)\,dt$ subject to $\dot k = f(k) - \delta k - c$. A standard textbook fact: its Euler equation (Keynes–Ramsey rule) for $u(c) = c^{1-\theta}/(1-\theta)$ is $\dot c/c = (f'(k) - \delta - \rho)/\theta$, and the steady state is a saddle point in the $(k,c)$ phase plane. Real business cycle models add aggregate shocks but keep a single representative agent, so there is no mean field yet.

When there are many agent types, the object is a **recursive competitive equilibrium**: policy functions together with a law of motion for the cross-sectional distribution, such that individual optimization and aggregate consistency hold at once. This is their name for the MFG fixed point.

### Translation table

| Economics | MFG |
|---|---|
| Recursive competitive equilibrium | Solution of the MFG system |
| Policy function | Optimal feedback control |
| Law of motion of the distribution | Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov forward) equation |
| Market clearing | Consistency condition on the mean field |
| Rational expectations | Agents' beliefs about the flow of measures are correct |
| Idiosyncratic vs. aggregate shock | Individual noise vs. common noise |
| Representative agent | No heterogeneity: the distribution is a point mass |
| Borrowing limit | State constraint (see [Bewley–Huggett–Aiyagari](#/note/bewley-huggett-aiyagari)) |
| Calibration | Choosing parameters to match moments of data |

Calibration has no analogue in PDE work. It is how economists validate a model, in the role that well-posedness plays for a mathematician.

### Suggested reading (from the chat)

Mas-Colell–Whinston–Green ch. 15–17 for general equilibrium vocabulary; Fudenberg–Tirole and Mas-Colell (1984) for the bridge through nonatomic games; Stokey–Lucas–Prescott for recursive language; Moll's lecture notes and Achdou–Han–Lasry–Lions–Moll (2022) for heterogeneous-agent models in continuous time; Krusell–Smith (1998) and a HANK survey for current macro.

## Cards

Q: In economics, what does "equilibrium" mean, and how does it differ from a steady state?
A: A fixed point: agents optimize taking as given something (prices, a distribution, strategies) that is itself produced by everyone's optimization. It need not be time-invariant; a dynamic equilibrium can be a whole path.
See: Equilibrium means fixed point

Q: What is the MFG analogue of the price vector in an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium?
A: The mean field. Prices clear markets given everyone's optimization, just as the flow of measures is regenerated by aggregating the controls that are optimal against it.
See: General equilibrium and the welfare theorems

Q: Which welfare theorem needs convexity, and why?
A: The second (every Pareto optimum is a competitive equilibrium after lump-sum transfers). Its proof separates the set of improving allocations by a price hyperplane. The first (every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient) needs only local nonsatiation.
See: General equilibrium and the welfare theorems

Q: How would an economist place MFG within their own literature?
A: As the dynamic, stochastic-control version of nonatomic games: a continuum of negligible players, each optimizing against the distribution of others' actions (Aumann, Schmeidler, Mas-Colell).
See: Nonatomic games: the economists' genealogy of MFG

Q: Read $\rho V(a) = \max_c \{u(c) + V'(a)(y + ra - c)\}$ as an MFG researcher.
A: A stationary discounted HJB. The state $a$ is wealth, the control $c$ is consumption, the drift is $y + ra - c$, $\rho$ is the discount rate, and the Hamiltonian is $\max_c\{u(c) - cV'\}$ plus the linear part $(y + ra)V'$.
See: Dynamic programming in the economist's dialect

Q: What is a **recursive competitive equilibrium**, in MFG terms?
A: Policy functions plus a law of motion for the cross-sectional distribution such that individual optimality and aggregate consistency hold simultaneously: the MFG fixed point (HJB + Fokker–Planck + consistency).
See: Dynamic programming in the economist's dialect

Q: Idiosyncratic shocks vs. aggregate shocks: what are they in MFG language?
A: Individual (independent) noise vs. common noise. Only aggregate shocks make the distribution random.
See: Translation table

Q: What is **calibration**, and what plays its role for a mathematician?
A: Choosing parameters so the model matches moments of real data. It is how economists validate a model, where a mathematician would prove well-posedness.
